Section 03
Forecasting Methodology

The model uses a Two-Step Weekly Average approach: first computing weekly case averages from historical data, then summing them into a monthly forecast. This method is transparent, auditable, and robust to missing data in individual years.

Step 1 — Weekly Forecast Average

For every combination of store, item, month, and week (1–5), the model identifies all historical years where that combination had non-zero orders, then computes the simple mean.

-- For each [store, item, month, week]: activeYears = { y ∈ [2020…2025] | orders(store, item, month, y) > 0 } weekForecast = mean( orders(store, item, month, week, y) for y in activeYears )
Why exclude zero-order years?

If a store did not carry a particular item in a given year, including that year's zero in the average would artificially deflate the forecast. The model excludes these years from the denominator, producing a more accurate estimate of expected demand when the item is available.

Step 2 — Monthly Forecast Sum

The monthly forecast is the sum of five weekly forecast averages. Each month is divided into five fixed windows:

Section 04
Weather Adjustment Model

Yogurtland sales are sensitive to weather conditions — rainy days reduce foot traffic and yogurt purchases. The model applies a data-driven weather adjustment to every weekly forecast using historical precipitation data matched to each store's location.

Rainy vs. Dry Classification

Each week is classified as Rainy or Dry based on total precipitation:

rainy = (weekly_precipitation_sum >= 0.10 inches)

Adjustment Logic — Three Pathways

Pathway 1 — Historical Rainy/Dry Average (Primary Method)

When at least 2 historical weeks in the same store, item, and month share the same rainy/dry classification as the 2026 forecast week:

matchingWeeks = { (store, item, month, week, year) | rainy_status matches 2026 } historicalAvg = mean( cases for week in matchingWeeks ) adjustment = historicalAvg − baseForecast -- Sanity guard: rainy adjustment must be negative; dry must be non-negative if rainy AND adjustment >= 0 → fall back to Pathway 2 if dry AND adjustment < 0 → adjustment = 0

Pathway 2 — Rain Fallback Drop Formula

Applied when rainy but insufficient matching history exists:

rainSeverity = max(0, precipitation − 0.10) dropRate = min(0.25, 0.10 + rainSeverity × 0.03) adjustment = −(baseForecast × dropRate) -- Examples: 0.10 in rain → 10% drop (0.10 + 0×0.03 = 10%) 0.40 in rain → 19% drop (0.10 + 0.3×0.03 = 19%) 0.73+ in rain → 25% drop (capped at maximum)

Pathway 3 — No Adjustment

Applied when: (a) week is Dry and no negative history exists, or (b) the store was not geocoded (9 stores).

Final Weather-Adjusted Forecast

weatherAdjusted = max(0, baseForecast + adjustment) -- Floor at zero — forecast cannot be negative
2025 Weather Analog

For 2026, the model uses 2025 same-month, same-week precipitation as the proxy for future weather. This is a practical and commonly-accepted approximation. As actual 2026 weather data becomes available (via real-time NOAA or Open-Meteo feeds), the adjustment can be re-run to produce updated order recommendations.

The ML model reduced WAPE from 72.3% to 41.1% — a 31.2 percentage point improvement. In absolute terms, the statistical model over-forecast by 73,531 cases (23.1% bias), while the ML model under-forecast by 46,984 cases (−14.7% bias). Incorporating additional signals (promotions, local events, store capacity) into the ML model is expected to further reduce error in future iterations.

Items with fewer than 2 years of history — primarily new seasonal or promo items — produce forecasts based on very limited data. These should be reviewed manually by the merchandising team before ordering.

No Economic or Foot Traffic Signals

The current model does not incorporate consumer price index trends, local population changes, or foot traffic data. Adding these signals is a key opportunity for the next-generation ML model.

Summary of Conclusions

  1. The 2026 forecast covers 301 stores, 81 flavors, and 190,670 unique store-item-month combinations, providing full coverage for all ordering decisions.
  2. The two-step weekly average model is a robust, transparent baseline that produces interpretable results, with outputs reviewable at the store and flavor level.
  3. The weather adjustment model adds meaningful accuracy, particularly in high-precipitation markets. Stores in the Pacific Northwest, Southeast, and Midwest benefit most.
  4. The ML model outperforms the statistical baseline by 31 percentage points in WAPE (41.1% vs 72.3%), and is the recommended primary model for 2026 ordering decisions.
  5. The forecast is most reliable for Core flavors at established stores with 4+ years of history. Seasonal and promo items warrant manual review.

Recommendations

R1 — Use ML Model as Primary, Statistical as Backstop

Deploy the ML model forecast as the primary order recommendation. Use the statistical average as a sanity-check backstop. Flag any rows where ML and statistical forecasts diverge by more than 30% for manual review.

R2 — Refresh Weather Adjustment Monthly

As each month of 2026 approaches, replace the 2025 analog weather data with the latest available forecast (NOAA 14-day or Open-Meteo forecast endpoint) to improve adjustment accuracy.

R3 — Manual Review for Seasonal Items

All seasonal and promo items with fewer than 3 years of history should be reviewed by the category management team before orders are finalized. Pair with sell-through data from POS systems.

R4 — Integrate Machine Learning for 2027 Forecast

Build a production ML forecasting pipeline incorporating promotional calendars, local events, consumer sentiment, and real-time POS sell-through data. See Section 12 for full ML implementation roadmap.

R5 — Geocode Remaining 9 Stores

Forecasting Using Machine Learning Algorithm

A practical guide to building a next-generation ML demand forecasting model for Yogurtland — replacing heuristic averages with data-driven predictions that learn from patterns, seasonality, promotions, and external signals.

1. Purpose of Machine Learning Forecasting

Traditional statistical forecasting methods — such as historical averages — are transparent and easy to audit, but they have fundamental limitations: they cannot learn non-linear patterns, they treat all years equally, and they cannot incorporate external signals like economic indicators or promotional calendars.

A machine learning forecasting model learns the underlying relationship between many input signals and actual demand outcomes. Once trained on historical data, it can generalize to new situations — a new store opening, a new flavor, an unusual weather event — with greater accuracy than any fixed formula.

3. Suggested Algorithms

Different algorithms have different strengths. The right choice depends on data volume, required interpretability, and infrastructure capabilities.

Linear Regression
Simple & Interpretable

Fits a straight-line relationship between features and demand. Fast to train, easy to explain to stakeholders, but cannot capture complex non-linear patterns. Best used as a benchmark.

Random Forest
Robust & Versatile

An ensemble of decision trees. Handles missing data, non-linear patterns, and feature interactions well. Provides feature importance scores. Recommended starting point for Yogurtland's dataset size.

XGBoost / LightGBM
High Performance

Gradient-boosted decision trees — the industry standard for tabular forecasting. Typically outperforms Random Forest with proper tuning. Used in the current Yogurtland ML model (ml flag in forecast_report_2026).

ARIMA / SARIMA
Time-Series Classic

Statistical time-series models that explicitly model trend and seasonality. Best suited for single time-series with strong seasonal patterns. Less scalable across 10,000+ store-item combinations.

Prophet (Meta)
Seasonality-Aware

Open-source library by Meta for decomposable time-series forecasting. Automatically handles weekly, monthly, and yearly seasonality plus holidays. Easy to use with limited ML expertise. Good for store-level series.

LSTM Neural Network
Advanced — Deep Learning

Long Short-Term Memory networks capture long-range temporal dependencies. Most powerful for complex multi-variate patterns but requires large amounts of data (3+ years per series), significant compute, and ML expertise.

Recommendation for Yogurtland: Start with XGBoost, Layer in Prophet

XGBoost on engineered tabular features (the approach already in use) is the right primary model given the dataset structure. Prophet can be layered in as a specialized model for high-volume Core flavors where seasonality decomposition adds interpretability. LSTM should be evaluated in 12–18 months once a richer feature set is assembled.

4. Step-by-Step ML Forecasting Process

  1. Collect Historical Data

    Pull all available purchase records (2020–2025) from the ERP system. Include every order line: store, item, date, quantity ordered, and quantity received. Supplement with POS sell-through data where available. The richer the history, the better the model's baseline understanding of demand patterns.

  2. Clean and Prepare the Data

    Remove duplicate transactions, correct data-entry errors, and resolve store name inconsistencies (e.g., applying store aliases like AZ1051 → AZ105). Handle missing weeks by deciding whether to impute zeros or flag as unavailable. Normalize units — ensure all quantities are in the same unit (cases). Split data into training (2020–2024) and validation (2025) sets.

  3. Create Features (Feature Engineering)

    Transform raw data into ML-ready input features. This is the most important step — good features drive good models.

    -- Calendar features month, week_of_month, quarter, is_holiday, days_to_holiday -- Lag features (historical demand signals) cases_lag_1week, cases_lag_4weeks, cases_lag_52weeks rolling_avg_4week, rolling_std_4week -- Item features category (Core/Rotator/Promo), flavor_type, is_seasonal, lto_week_number -- Store features store_region, store_age_years, store_format -- Weather features precipitation_mm, avg_temp_f, is_rainy, heat_index -- Promotion features is_promo_active, promo_discount_pct, days_into_promo
  4. Train the Machine Learning Model

    Fit the chosen algorithm (e.g., XGBoost) on the training dataset (2020–2024). Use cross-validation across multiple time folds to prevent overfitting. Tune hyperparameters (learning rate, tree depth, number of estimators) using grid search or Bayesian optimization. Separate models can be trained per item group (Flavor vs. Topping) or per category (Core vs. Seasonal) for better specialization.

  5. Test Model Accuracy

    Evaluate the trained model on the 2025 hold-out validation set. Compute MAE, RMSE, WAPE, and Bias metrics at multiple granularities: overall, per store, per flavor category, and per month. Compare against the statistical baseline. Identify which stores or items have the highest error rates and investigate root causes (insufficient history, unusual events, data quality issues).

  6. Forecast Future Sales

    Apply the trained model to 2026 feature inputs to generate weekly and monthly case forecasts. For future weeks, use the 2025 weather analog (or live weather forecast API for near-term months). Apply any known 2026 promotional calendar flags. Generate confidence intervals (prediction intervals) so planners know the range of uncertainty around each forecast.

  7. Compare Actual vs. Forecast (Ongoing Monitoring)

    As each 2026 month closes, compare actual orders against the forecast. Track WAPE and Bias trends over time. Feed actual data back into a rolling retraining pipeline so the model continuously improves. Flag stores or items where actual demand diverges from forecast by more than 25% for root-cause investigation and manual override.

5. Benefits of Machine Learning for Forecasting

Higher Accuracy

ML models learn complex, non-linear relationships in data that fixed formulas cannot capture. Back-testing shows 31+ percentage point WAPE improvement over the statistical baseline — translating directly to fewer stockouts and less waste.

Automatic Seasonality Detection

ML models automatically discover seasonal and cyclical patterns across all 81 flavors and 301 stores, without requiring manual seasonality flags for every product.

Scalability

Once the model is built, running forecasts for 190,000+ store-item-month combinations takes seconds. Scaling to new stores or new items simply requires adding rows to the feature dataset — no model redesign needed.

Incorporates External Signals

Unlike simple averages, ML models can learn the effect of promotions, holidays, weather, and economic conditions simultaneously — producing demand-aware forecasts that adapt to the real world.

Continuous Improvement

A retraining pipeline means the model improves every month as new actuals are added. The model naturally adapts to long-term trend shifts (e.g., growth in plant-based flavors) without manual recalibration.

Feature Importance Insights

ML models (especially tree-based ones) expose which features drive demand. This gives category managers and supply chain analysts actionable intelligence — e.g., "temperature is the #1 driver for sorbet sales."
